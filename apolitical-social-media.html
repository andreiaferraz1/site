/ The Challenge
N. 02
A user-friendly system empowering Apolitical team to create cohesive, on-brand social media content at scale.
Apolitical needed to solve a structural problem. The marketing team depended on design for every LinkedIn post, which slowed down publishing and created visual inconsistency whenever someone tried to adapt content without design support.

I led the research and design process for this project at Babco, working under the supervision of the Babco's founder, Olivia Batraski. My job wasn't just to make things look good. It was to design a system that people without a design background could actually run on their own, without breaking the brand.

That meant making real trade-off decisions: where to give freedom (accent colors, image choice) and where to lock things down (typographic hierarchy, grid, proportions), always treating the marketing team as the actual user of the system, not just the LinkedIn audience.

3 things guided every decision:
the system had to work for non-designers first, stay recognizably Apolitical no matter who used it, and scale without needing design rework for each new piece of content.

/ Navigating Ambiguity
N. 03
When I joined the project, Apolitical had already gone through several attempts on LinkedIn social posts and visual identity, none of which had worked. The problem wasn't lack of effort. It was lack of alignment. Each attempt tried to fix the surface (style, colors, templates) without first answering the right question about what the system actually needed to solve.

Before proposing any visual direction, I ran a structured discovery process. I looked through the content history on LinkedIn and the client's other social channels, and put together a discovery brief around the criteria I felt were essential to get to a result that actually matched the business need, not just another aesthetic. The decision was to prioritize function before form: figure out how the system needed to behave first, and let that shape the visual standard, instead of the other way around, which is what had been tried up to that point.

A concrete example of this: the client came in with a long, fragmented list of content categories. Instead of trying to account for all of them visually, I restructured it, grouping and absorbing subcategories into the content types the team actually used regularly: Events, News, Courses, Article, Newsletter, Hiring, Announcements. That simplification became the foundation the visual components were built on, so each category had a clear, recognizable pattern instead of a one-off visual exception for every variation.

That shift in approach is what broke the cycle of fragmented attempts and got the project to a system with real consistency in style, components, patterns, and objectives.

/ Design SYSTEM
N. 05
Instead of delivering a set of static templates, I built the system around a proper component library: a foundations layer covering Logo, Colors, Typography, Iconography, Badge, Signature Tile, and LinkedIn Tile, with master components reused as instances across 934 individual placements throughout the templates. Updating a master component propagates that change across the whole system.

Since the people actually using this day to day were non-designers, the library ships with governance built directly into the file. There's a dedicated Foundations page documenting how components and instances relate, why deleting a master component breaks every instance built on it, when to use "Detach Instance" for one-off exceptions, and naming conventions to keep the library organized as it grows. That's what turns the library into a real single source of truth the marketing team can rely on without needing a designer to step in.

The system also includes an onboarding layer, a "Get Started" page explaining what the file is for and how to use it, which doubled as a bridge between the agency and the client's marketing team, keeping both sides working off the same assets and process.

On top of the foundations, the library covers 10 content categories the marketing team actually publishes regularly: News, Courses, Keywords, 100 Lists, Article, Newsletter, Event, Hiring, Announcements, and Communities of Practice, each with several validated template variations. Contextual guidance sits right next to each variation (text/image contrast rules, when to use a background image versus a solid color, how to swap logos or images), and multi-frame carousel posts follow a defined pattern: the logo shows up only on the first and last frame, with an arrow motif signaling continuity through the middle frames.

That combination, governed components, embedded documentation, and an onboarding layer for non-designers, is what made the system genuinely self-service.

/ Component Guidelines
N. 08
Adaptive Components
Designed as a flexible Figma component system, each element includes purposeful variants for different content and format needs—giving teams freedom without compromising consistency.
Logo to arrow
In carousel posts, the Apolitical logo can be replaced by a directional arrow in the same fixed position. This preserves the layout rhythm while creating a clear visual cue to continue through the story.
01 / FORMAT LOGIC
Light or dark
Logo and arrow components include light and dark versions. Designers select the variant that best contrasts with the image or color beneath it, ensuring reliable visibility across every composition.
02 / CONTRAST LOGIC
One badge, many categories
The badge follows the same modular logic. Content type, label, and icon can change while structure, alignment, and spacing remain fixed—making every category easy to identify and every post unmistakably part of the same system.

/ Final Result
N. 10
The redesigned social media system strengthened brand consistency, improved engagement, and enabled scalable, high-quality content creation across teams.
The system changed how Apolitical's marketing team produces content. Posts no longer needed design approval or direct intervention for every single piece, and internal feedback from the team pointed to more confidence and speed in production. Visually, the LinkedIn identity became consistently recognizable, a difference you can see clearly in the direct before-and-after comparison.

Design consistency and content velocity were within the scope of this system. Social engagement outcomes depend on content strategy and paid media, and aren't claimed as a result of this work.
